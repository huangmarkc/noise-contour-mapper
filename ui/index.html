Noise Contour Mapper — User Guide

This tool turns your sound level meter readings into a color-coded noise map drawn over a floor plan. Each area is colored by its noise level — for example, red for 90–95 dBA — and contour lines mark the boundaries between levels, such as the 85 dBA line. Use it to plan hearing-conservation zones, find loud areas, and explain noise risk to others.

Your data stays with you. Nothing you load or type is uploaded or stored anywhere. Closing or reloading the page erases your work, so save your project (step 10) before you leave.

Before you start

You will need:

  • A floor plan as an image (PNG or JPG) or a PDF. A clean, uncluttered plan is easiest to work on.
  • Your sound level meter readings in dBA, and where on the plan each one was taken.
  • One known distance on the plan, such as a dimensioned wall or a column grid spacing. This lets the tool work in meters or feet.
  • Optional: the main noisy machines and their sound level at a known distance, for example “92 dBA at 1 m” from a datasheet or a close-up reading.
New to the tool? Click Load demo (sidebar, section 1) to open a finished example with readings, three machines, and walls with doorways. Try changing the settings to see what they do.

The numbered sections down the left side follow the order of the work, from top to bottom. The buttons above the map are the drawing tools.

Step 1 — Load the floor plan (section 1)

  1. Click Load floor plan…
  2. Choose your PNG, JPG or PDF file. For a PDF, only the first page is used.

Moving around the plan: roll the mouse wheel to zoom in and out. With Pan / select chosen in the toolbar, click and drag an empty part of the plan to move it. Click Fit to see the whole plan again.

Step 2 — Set the scale (section 6)

Setting the scale tells the tool how many pixels on the plan equal one meter (or foot). It adds a scale bar and shows positions in real units. You need it for noise sources, for walls that block or reflect sound, and for partial barriers.

  1. Click Calibrate scale… (or Calibrate in the toolbar).
  2. Click one end of the known distance, then the other end. Zoom in first for better accuracy.
  3. Type the real distance, choose meters or feet, and click Set scale.
  4. Optional: click Set origin (0,0)… and click a reference point, such as a building corner. Positions are then shown as distances to the right (x) and upward (y) from that point — handy if you measured positions on site with a tape or laser.

Step 3 — Add your measurements (section 2)

  1. Click Add points in the toolbar.
  2. Click the plan where a reading was taken.
  3. Type the reading in dBA and press Enter. A label is filled in for you; change it if you like.
  4. Repeat for every reading. The map appears as soon as there are two readings, and it updates as you go.

To fix a mistake: change the value or label in the list in section 2, or click ✕ to delete it. To move a reading, choose Pan / select and drag its marker.

To import from a spreadsheet: save it as a CSV file with the columns label, x, y, dBA (or just x, y, dBA). In section 2, choose what the x and y numbers are — pixels, percent of the plan, or meters/feet — then click Import CSV. For meters/feet, set the scale and the origin first, and measure x to the right and y upward from that origin.

Step 4 — Draw walls and barriers (section 2c)

Walls change how sound travels. Drawing them lets the map show a quiet office next to a loud press room, instead of blending the two together. Walls do three things:

  • They block sound passing through them.
  • They reflect sound, making it louder near hard walls.
  • They stop measurements “leaking” into the next room. A reading in the press room only affects the office through a doorway.

How to draw a wall

  1. In section 2c, choose the Type for new walls (see the table below).
  2. Click Draw wall in the toolbar, or press W.
  3. Click where the wall starts, then click at each corner along it. Each click finishes one straight section and starts the next.
  4. Double-click, or press Esc, to finish the wall. Your next click starts a new wall.

Drawing tips

  • Leave a gap for every doorway, open bay or other opening. Sound, and the influence of your readings, passes through gaps but not through walls. If a door is usually kept shut, you can draw across it instead.
  • Hold Shift to draw a perfectly straight horizontal or vertical line.
  • Let corners snap. When the pointer comes close to the end of a wall, or to a wall itself, it jumps onto it and a pink circle appears. Snapped walls join without gaps. Even a tiny gap at a corner lets sound through.
  • You don’t need every wall. Draw the walls between areas with different noise levels, and solid walls close to loud machines (because of reflections). Walls in quiet areas far from machines make little difference.

Changing and removing walls

  • Select a wall: with Pan / select chosen, click the wall on the map. It turns blue, and its row in section 2c is highlighted. You can also click its row.
  • Move an end: with Pan / select chosen, drag the round handle at the wall’s end. Walls joined at that corner move together, so the corner stays closed.
  • Change the type: pick a new type in the wall’s row. Its values reset to that type’s defaults.
  • Fine-tune: change Blocks (dB), Absorbs (%) or Height in the wall’s row.
  • Delete: select the wall and press Delete, or click ✕ in its row. Remove all walls clears every wall.

What the numbers mean:

  • Blocks — how much quieter sound is after passing through the wall. A 10 dB drop sounds roughly half as loud. These are real-world values. They are lower than laboratory ratings because doors, gaps, and sound travelling through floors and ceilings let some noise past in real buildings.
  • Absorbs — the share of sound the wall soaks up instead of bouncing back. Hard surfaces absorb almost nothing, so they reflect almost everything.
  • Height — partial barriers only. The taller the barrier compared with the machine and the listener, the more it helps.

On the map: dark solid line = wall  ·  teal line = sound-absorbing wall (absorbs 30% or more)  ·  brown dashed line = partial barrier.

Step 5 — Draw rooms for reverberation (optional, section 2d)

In a hard room — bare concrete, metal, few soft surfaces — sound bounces around and builds up. The further you are from a machine, the more of what you hear is this bounced, reverberant sound, so levels stop dropping off with distance. Drawing rooms lets the source model add it. It also lets sound from a loud room spill into the rooms next to it through doorways.

How to draw a room

  1. Make sure the scale is set (step 2) and the walls are drawn (step 4).
  2. Click Draw room in the toolbar, or press R.
  3. Click each corner of the room, going around it. Corners snap onto walls, wall ends and other rooms’ corners (a pink circle appears).
  4. To close the room, click the first corner again, or double-click. Press Esc to cancel a room you are drawing.
  5. Set the room’s Finish and Ceiling height in section 2d.

Drawing tips

  • Trace each room along its walls, and let neighboring rooms share their edges. The tool finds the openings between two rooms by looking for gaps in the walls along their shared edge.
  • Doorways: a gap of up to 8 m in the walls between two rooms counts as a doorway of the Doorway height (section 3, default 3 m). A wider gap counts as open plan, open to the full ceiling height.
  • An edge of a room with no wall and no neighboring room counts as a solid wall of that room.
  • Rooms shouldn’t overlap.

Room settings

  • Name — shown on the map.
  • Finish — how much sound the room’s surfaces soak up on average. Pick the closest description (see the table).
  • Ceiling — the ceiling height, used for the room’s volume and surface area.
  • Absorbs — the average absorption itself, if you want to fine-tune it.
  • Reverb. time — the reverberation time (T60): how many seconds a sound takes to die away after it stops. The tool calculates it from the room. If you have measured it, type your value in, and the tool sets the absorption to match.

Changing rooms: with Pan / select chosen, click a room’s name on the map to select it (it turns blue). Drag its round corner handles to reshape it, or press Delete to remove it. You can also click its row in section 2d.

Step 6 — Add noise sources (optional, section 2b)

Noise sources are the machines you know about. With them, the tool can calculate noise from physics — including walls and reflections — instead of only filling in between your readings.

  1. Make sure the scale is set (step 2).
  2. Click Add source in the toolbar, then click the machine’s location.
  3. Enter its sound level and the distance it applies at, for example 92 dBA at 1 m. Click Add.
  4. In section 3, set Method to Source model + residuals.

Sources appear as orange diamonds. Drag them with Pan / select, and change their levels in section 2b.

Other settings:

  • IDW power — higher makes each reading dominate its own area; lower gives a smoother, more averaged map. Also used to spread the corrections in the source model.
  • Grid resolution — higher gives finer detail but takes longer to calculate.
  • Mask beyond — hides areas farther than this many pixels from every reading, so you don’t show guesses for areas you didn’t survey. 0 turns it off.

Wall settings

  • Measurements don’t pass through walls (all methods) — a reading only affects places it can reach by going around walls, such as through a doorway. The longer the way round, the less it counts. A room closed off from every reading is left blank (“no data”) rather than guessed.
  • Walls block sound (source model) — sound through a wall is reduced by its Blocks value. Sound also travels around wall ends (through doorways) and over partial barriers, reduced the more it has to bend.
  • Walls reflect sound (source model) — hard walls bounce sound back, raising levels near them: about 3 dB beside a hard wall, more in corners. Sound-absorbing walls reflect much less.
  • Machine height and Ear height — used only for partial barriers. The defaults are 1.0 m for the noisy part of the machine and 1.5 m for a standing worker’s ear.

Room settings

  • Room reverberation (source model) — adds each room’s reverberant sound, as described in step 5.
  • Doorway height — the height of the doorways between rooms (gaps up to 8 m wide). Taller doorways let more sound through.
Tip: switch each wall and room setting off and on to see how much it changes the map. This also helps you explain the effect of walls and rooms to others.

Step 8 — Adjust how the map looks (sections 4 and 5)

  • Heat map style — Banded gives one solid color per noise band; Smooth gradient blends between them.
  • Overlay opacity — how see-through the colors are, so the plan shows underneath.
  • Checkboxes — show or hide contour lines, contour labels, point markers, dBA labels, the legend, the walls and the rooms.
  • Noise bands (section 5) — the defaults are below 80, 80–85, 85–90, 90–95 and above 95 dBA. Type a new number to move a boundary, click a color to change it, or use + Add band, − Remove top band and Reset. A contour line is drawn at every boundary.

Step 9 — Read the map

  • Each color shows the estimated noise band (see the legend). The numbered contour lines mark the boundaries, for example the line labeled 85.
  • Move the mouse over the map. The bar at the bottom shows the position and the estimated level at that spot.
  • A blank (uncolored) area has no estimate. Either it is beyond the Mask distance, or walls close it off from every reading.

Step 10 — Save and export (section 7)

  • Map title — printed on the exported map.
  • Save project — saves everything (plan, readings, sources, walls, rooms and settings) in one .json file. Click Open project later to carry on exactly where you left off.
  • Export map as PNG — an image for reports, with the legend, scale bar, title, date and calculation method.
  • Export CSV (section 2) — your readings as a spreadsheet file.

Each time, you choose where the file goes, such as your Desktop or your own cloud drive. In Chrome and Edge a Save window opens. Firefox and Safari put the file in your downloads folder instead.

Nothing is stored for you. If you close or reload the page without saving, your work is gone. The page warns you if you try to leave with unsaved changes.

How walls and rooms change the calculation

In the source model, sound can reach a spot from each machine in up to four ways. The tool works out each one:

  1. Straight through. Sound loses strength with distance (6 dB quieter each time the distance doubles). Each wall it passes through takes off that wall’s Blocks value.
  2. Around a wall, for example through a doorway. The tool finds the shortest way around the wall ends. The longer the detour compared with the straight line, the more the sound is reduced, up to 20 dB.
  3. Over a partial barrier. Sound bends over the top. The reduction depends on how far the barrier rises above the line between the machine and the listener’s ear, again up to 20 dB.
  4. Reflected off a wall. Each wall acts like a mirror and bounces each machine’s sound back once. Sound-absorbing walls bounce back less.

With rooms drawn, a fifth part is added:

  1. Reverberation. Inside each room, the tool adds the bounced sound that builds up, using the standard room-acoustics formula. It depends on the machines’ sound power and on the room’s surface area and absorption. It is the same everywhere in the room, so far from the machines it keeps levels from dropping away. Rooms also pass sound to each other: each room’s reverberant sound spills through the doorways and, much less, through the walls into the rooms next to it, and direct sound arriving from machines in other rooms adds to a room’s reverberation too.

All of these are added the way sound adds: two equal sounds together are 3 dB louder, not twice the number. Finally, the whole map is adjusted so it matches your readings exactly. That adjustment also covers effects the tool doesn’t model, and it too goes around walls rather than through them. With rooms drawn, each room is adjusted by its own readings; a room without readings borrows its neighbors’ adjustment.

For IDW and Radial basis, walls don’t change any acoustics. They only stop readings influencing the far side of a wall, except through openings.

Limits and good practice

The tool keeps the physics simple so that it is quick and easy to check. It does not model:

  • Sound bouncing off two or more walls in a row, or off the ceiling and floor.
  • Uneven reverberation. The reverberant sound is taken as evenly spread through each room. In very long, low halls it really fades slowly with distance, so far from the machines the map may read a little high. Rooms you don’t draw get no reverberation at all.
  • Pitch. Everything is in single dBA values, but real walls and barriers block high-pitched noise better than low rumble.
  • The size of machines. Each machine is treated as a single point; long machines and conveyors are really spread out.
  • Changing noise. Steady noise is assumed; impacts, tones and on/off cycles are not modeled.

Adjusting the map to your readings makes up for much of this near the places you measured.

Good practice. The map is an estimate for planning and communication. Confirm important boundaries — especially the 85 dBA action level — with extra measurements. The map does not replace personal noise dosimetry or a regulatory exposure assessment.
